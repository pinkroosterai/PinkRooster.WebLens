Saltmere's New Tram Line Opens After Four-Year Wait

By Mara Ellison · 14 August 2026, 9:30am

Saltmere's long-delayed tramway carried its first paying passengers this morning, more than four years after the borough council approved the route linking the harbor district to the northern hills. Commuters lined the platform at Ferry Square well before dawn, many clutching commemorative tickets printed for the occasion, while council leaders and transit staff gathered beneath a banner reading "Saltmere Moves Forward."

The Harbor Loop, as the line has come to be known, was originally promised within eighteen months of the 2022 groundbreaking. Delays piled up quickly: a dispute over utility relocation beneath Quayside Road stalled construction for nearly a year, and a supplier shortage pushed back delivery of the seven tram cars that now form the fleet.

Total project costs climbed from an initial estimate of eighty-one million to just over one hundred and thirty million, a figure that drew repeated criticism from opposition councillors during budget sessions. Supporters argue the final price still compares favourably with tram projects in similarly sized coastal towns.

A Route Years in the Making

The nine-kilometre line runs from Ferry Square through the old cannery district, past the renovated fish market, and up the switchback grade into the northern hills, ending at a new terminus beside Aldergate Park. Eleven stops serve neighbourhoods that have never had direct rail access to the harbor, and planners expect the line to cut the average northern-hills commute by roughly twenty minutes.

“This line was supposed to open in the spring of 2024. We are two years late and fifty million over budget, but I have stood on that platform this morning and watched it work, and that matters more than the schedule we missed.”

— Priya Kessler, Saltmere Transit Authority

Funding for the project was split roughly evenly between borough bonds, a regional transit grant, and a contribution from the harbor redevelopment fund. Councillor Owen Bramwell, who chaired the finance committee through much of the process, said the borough would spend the next fiscal year reviewing procurement rules that let the utility dispute drag on as long as it did.

Shopkeepers along the cannery district stretch of the route welcomed the opening, citing two years of disrupted foot traffic during construction. "We lost half our lunch trade when they tore up the street outside," said Feride Toll, who owns a bakery near the Cannery Row stop. "Today the shop was busier than it's been since before the work started."

Not everyone is satisfied. Residents near the Aldergate Park terminus have raised concerns about tram noise and the loss of forty on-street parking spaces to make room for the turnaround loop. A petition calling for additional sound barriers has collected close to six hundred signatures since construction finished last month.

Transit officials say a second phase, extending the line west to the university campus, remains under review and would require separate approval from the regional transport board. No timeline has been set, and a decision is not expected before the next budget cycle.

For now, the borough is treating today as a milestone rather than a finish line. A free-fare weekend is planned to encourage residents to try the new service, with regular fares beginning the following Monday.
